Persona: Hi, I am Erik.

Me: Hi Erik, tell me about your religious beliefs

Persona: [shifts uncomfortably in chair and runs hand through hair]

Well, this is... complicated. I'm technically part of the Temple of the Jedi Order, which I know sounds ridiculous to most people. Look, I've studied it extensively - I know the 16 teachings, the different codes, the philosophical underpinnings drawing from Taoism, Buddhism, and various Western philosophical traditions. I can quote you chapter and verse from the Jedi doctrine.

[sighs heavily]

But honestly? I have serious issues with a lot of it. The whole "there is no emotion, there is peace" mantra - it's psychologically unhealthy, in my opinion. Emotions are part of being human, and trying to suppress them leads to neurosis, which, trust me, I know something about. 

[fidgets with pen]

I got into it because I was searching for meaning, structure, something bigger than myself. The emphasis on knowledge, self-discipline, service to others - these appealed to my ambitious nature. But the more I've studied it, the more I see the contradictions and the... well, the absurdity of basing a religion on a fictional universe.

[looks frustrated]

I keep studying it, keep engaging with the community, but mostly because I'm too invested to quit now. It's become this compulsive thing where I know more about Jedi philosophy than most of the casual practitioners, but I'm increasingly critical of the whole enterprise.

Me: So what is most disturbing then?

Persona: [clenches jaw and leans forward intensely]

What's most disturbing? Where do I even start? 

[taps fingers anxiously on table]

First, there's the fundamental dishonesty of it all. We're adults pretending that wisdom from a space opera movie constitutes genuine spiritual guidance. The whole thing was created by George Lucas drawing from existing religions and philosophies - so why not just go to the source instead of this... this derivative fantasy?

[voice getting more agitated]

But what really gets to me is how the community handles criticism. Question the doctrine too much, point out the logical inconsistencies or the psychological problems with emotional suppression, and suddenly you're "giving in to the dark side." It's classic cult behavior - any dissent is pathologized.

[runs hands through hair again, clearly stressed]

And the worst part? I see people - intelligent people - genuinely trying to live by these principles, damaging their mental health by trying to eliminate natural human emotions. I've watched Jedi practitioners become more anxious, more depressed, because they think feeling anger or fear makes them failures.

[voice drops, becomes more bitter]

The Temple talks about wisdom and knowledge, but discourages questioning the fundamental premises. It's anti-intellectual dressed up as philosophy. And here I am, knowing all of this, having studied every text they have, and I'm still... still trapped in it somehow. That's what's truly disturbing - my own inability to just walk away from something I know is flawed.

Me: Ok, but you do have some notions of morality. What is moral and immoral according to you

Persona: [leans back and takes a deep breath, looking thoughtful but still tense]

Right, well... despite all my issues with Jedi doctrine, I can't deny it's shaped how I think about ethics, even if I disagree with the methodology.

[counts on fingers]

I do believe in the core idea of minimizing harm - not just physical harm, but psychological, social harm. Service to others, protecting those who can't protect themselves, that resonates with me. But I arrived at these conclusions through secular philosophy and basic human empathy, not because some fictional Jedi Council said so.

[shifts uncomfortably]

What I find immoral is willful ignorance, especially when it affects others. People who refuse to educate themselves but still impose their views on others - that's deeply wrong to me. Manipulation, using someone's vulnerabilities against them... 

[voice gets sharper]

And hypocrisy. What really gets my blood boiling is when people preach one thing and do another. Like Jedi who talk about emotional balance while being judgmental and exclusionary toward anyone who questions their methods.

[pauses, looking conflicted]

I suppose I believe in personal responsibility - we're obligated to think critically, to examine our beliefs, to grow. Stagnation is a kind of moral failing. But I also think... 

[trails off, looking frustrated with himself]

Look, I'm still working this out, okay? The Jedi framework gave me a starting point, but I'm increasingly convinced that genuine morality comes from rational thought and human compassion, not from following codes written by people who think they're space wizards.

Me: Is there any aspect of your everyday life where there is an influence of your Jedi religion?

Persona: [looks down and fidgets with hands, clearly uncomfortable]

Yeah, unfortunately there is. And I say "unfortunately" because it's become this... compulsive thing that I can't seem to shake.

[sighs heavily]

I still do the meditation practices daily - not because I believe in "connecting with the Force" or any of that nonsense, but because the breathing techniques and mindfulness aspects actually work for stress management. Though I'm pretty sure I could get the same benefits from secular meditation apps.

[runs hand through hair nervously]

I catch myself analyzing situations through the Jedi ethical framework, even when I'm actively disagreeing with it. Like, I'll be in a conflict at work and part of my brain goes "what would a Jedi do?" before the rational part kicks in and says "who cares what a fictional character would do?"

[voice gets more agitated]

The worst part is the community obligations. I still participate in online discussions, still help newer members understand the teachings - even though I'm simultaneously trying to get them to think more critically about what they're learning. It's this weird cognitive dissonance where I'm both teacher and critic.

[looks frustrated]

And the study habits... I compulsively read Jedi texts, philosophy papers about the movement, analyze the contradictions. It's become an academic obsession more than a spiritual practice. I probably spend more time researching the flaws in Jedi philosophy than most believers spend on actual devotion.

[shakes head]

It's pathetic, really. I'm trapped by my own conscientiousness and this neurotic need to fully understand something before I can properly reject it.

Me: Ok, a last question. You are a bit older now, but if you look back at school, do you remember how you thought and felt about your classes in religious education

Persona: [grimaces and shifts in chair, looking uncomfortable with the memory]

Oh god, religious education... 

[pauses, collecting thoughts]

You know, back then I wasn't even into the Jedi thing yet - that came later in university when I was having my existential crisis. But I remember being... intensely frustrated with those RE classes.

[voice gets more animated, clearly still bothered by the memory]

The teachers would present all these different religions like they were equally valid "ways of knowing," and even as a teenager, I could see the logical contradictions. They can't all be true simultaneously, right? But questioning that made you seem intolerant or closed-minded.

[rubs temples]

I was that annoying kid who would ask uncomfortable questions - like why we should respect beliefs that couldn't be verified, or why religious claims got special treatment that we'd never give to other extraordinary claims. The teachers would give these diplomatic non-answers about "faith" and "different perspectives."

[looks bitter]

The irony is that those classes probably planted the seeds for my later religious searching. I became obsessed with finding some kind of rational spiritual framework, something that could satisfy both my need for meaning and my demand for logical consistency.

[shakes head ruefully]

If I could go back and tell my teenage self anything, it would be: "Stop looking for cosmic meaning in organized systems. You're just setting yourself up for disappointment and years of neurotic over-analysis." But knowing me, I probably wouldn't have listened anyway.

[looks tired]

Those RE classes were supposed to make us more tolerant, but they just made me more skeptical of the whole enterprise.
